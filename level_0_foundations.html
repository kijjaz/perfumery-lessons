2 The 4 Physical Building Blocks

How raw materials transform step-by-step into a commercial finished flacon:

Level 1 Unit
Raw Material

The foundational discrete ingredient on the organ: whether a pure isolate (PEA), a synthetic reaction mixture of isomers (Iso E Super, Cashmeran), or a complex natural extract.

e.g. Bergamot FCF, Rose (PEA), Iso E Super, Coumarin
Level 2 Unit (1 + 1 = 3)
Accord (Chord)

2 to 4 materials balanced so precisely they fuse into an indivisible third identity.

e.g. Lavender + Coumarin = Shaving Soap
Level 3 Unit
Specialty Base

A complex, pre-compounded micro-formula used as a modular brick inside larger perfumes.

e.g. Ambre 83, De Laire Mousse de Saxe
Level 4 Unit
Finished Fragrance

The complete 100.0-part concentrate diluted in 96% alcohol to an exact concentration standard.

e.g. 15% EdP, 10% EdT, 5% Cologne

3 The 3 Levels of Scent Taxonomy

Never confuse an **odor facet** with a **historical archetype** or an overarching **fragrance family**:

Tier 1 • Granular Perception
Odor Facet & Descriptors

The specific sensory adjectives you perceive at the smelling strip. A single molecule often has 3 to 4 distinct facets.

• Coumarin: Toasted almond, sweet hay, warm marzipan
• IBQ: Green peat, smoked hide, rubber
Tier 2 • Historical Blueprint
Fragrance Archetype

The landmark historical formula skeletons that founded entire traditions through chemical breakthroughs.

• 1882 Fougère Royale (Lavender + Coumarin)
• 1917 Chypre de Coty (Bergamot + Oakmoss)
• 1925 Shalimar (Amber & Ethyl Vanillin)
Tier 3 • Macro Classification
Fragrance Family

The broad commercial genus grouping perfumes by dominant structural behavior and market territory.

• Citrus / Cologne
• Aromatic Fougère
• Chypre & Leather
• Amber (Classical Balsamic)

Student Foundations FAQ: Key Questions & Answers

Common doubts and misconceptions beginners have when stepping into perfumery:

1. Is it possible to build a real perfume if I haven't studied raw materials or mixing before?

Yes, absolutely! Traditional perfumery schools often force students to memorize 100+ raw materials for 6 months before touching a formula, leading to sensory fatigue and frustration. In our Progressive Spiral Curriculum, you learn to formulate immediately at Level 1 with only 5 bottles. Because those 5 materials represent the essential structural poles (Citrus Top, Floral Heart, Cedar Backbone, Vanilla Sweetness, and Green Dew), you can compound a balanced, complete 15% Eau de Parfum on your very first day. You don't need to know all materials to compose great perfume—you only need to understand the structural role of the materials in front of you.

2. Why can't I just mix materials that smell good together? Why follow the 5-stage life cycle?

Perfume is dynamic chemistry across space and time, not a static painting. Mixing randomly creates three severe failures:
• Olfactory Mud & Vanishing Scent: Unstructured materials clash for the exact same evaporation window, canceling each other out, or evaporate in 20 minutes without an architectural backbone (Hedione, Iso E Super).
• Answering the Brief — What to Express (Stage 2): Before asking "How do I formulate?", you must answer the client's brief: What do we want to express? (e.g. An intimate cashmere cocoon of quiet luxury vs. an electrifying morning dewdrop). Stage 2 defines the desired emotional experience, tactile texture (powdery, dewy, velvety), and technical targets (sillage throw, 8h+ longevity).
• The 'How' Lives in Mind-Mapping & Formulation (Stages 3 & 4): While technical awareness starts in Stage 2 (noticing potential discoloration like Vanillin browning or maceration shifts), the actual technical 'how' happens during Mind-Mapping (Stage 3) and Formulation (Stage 4)—where you select specific molecules to carry the idea, prevent chemical clashes, and balance 100.0 parts by weight on the analytical scale.
Following the 5-Stage Life Cycle ensures you know what to express before committing your formulas to the bench.

3. What is the real difference between an Accord, a Specialty Base, and a Finished Fragrance?

Think of them as modular physical units:
• A Raw Material is the foundational discrete ingredient on your bench. It can be a single chemical isolate (e.g. Phenylethyl alcohol, Coumarin), a synthetic chemical mixture of isomers/congeners synthesized together (e.g. Iso E Super, Hedione, Cashmeran, Ethylene Brassylate), or a complex multi-component botanical oil or resin (e.g. Bergamot FCF, Labdanum).
• An Accord is a balanced micro-chassis of 2 to 4 raw materials fused into an indivisible third identity (e.g. Lavender + Coumarin = clean barbershop shaving lather).
• A Specialty Base is a pre-compounded complex accord (like Ambre 83) created by fragrance houses as an off-the-shelf structural brick so perfumers don't have to rebuild complex ambers or vintage mosses from scratch in every formula.
• A Finished Fragrance is the completed 100.0-part architectural symphony containing top, heart, and base accords, diluted in 96% alcohol (e.g., 15% Eau de Parfum).

4. Why do professional perfumers formulate out of parts (100, 1,000, or 10,000) instead of counting drops?

Counting 'drops' is unpredictable and unscientific: viscosity and surface tension vary radically across materials. A cold, sticky drop of Labdanum resinoid can weigh 3 to 4 times more than a warm, runny drop of Bergamot citrus oil! If you formulate in drops, your perfume can never be replicated accurately.

Instead, professional perfumery formulates strictly in parts by weight on analytical digital balances ($0.001\text{g}$ precision):
• 100.0 Parts Total (% by weight): The pedagogical gold standard used in our course. Ratios translate directly into intuitive percentages, making it effortless to balance structure and audit IFRA limits.
• 1,000 Parts Total (Permille standard): The traditional European fragrance house standard (Givaudan, Firmenich, IFF). Scaling out of 1,000 allows perfumers to specify trace materials down to whole numbers (e.g., 2 parts out of 1,000 rather than 0.2%) without drowning in decimal points.
• 10,000 Parts Total (Ultra-trace industrial standard): Used for complex commercial fine fragrances containing hyper-potent captives, pyrazines, sulfur compounds, or high-impact trace aldehydes where an ingredient must be precisely controlled down to parts-per-ten-thousand ($0.01\%$).

Whether using 100, 1,000, or 10,000 parts, mass-based formulation allows you to instantly scale a trial from a 5.00g bench vial to a 10.00g flacon, or a 500-kilogram industrial batch with zero mathematical error.

5. Why do some materials need to be diluted to 10% or 1% before mixing? Which solvents should I use, and what are the myths?

Aroma chemicals differ by orders of magnitude in their olfactory impact thresholds. Ultra-powerful molecules like Cis-3-Hexenol (green leaf stem) or Isobutyl Quinoline / IBQ (peaty leather) will completely paralyze the nose and ruin a blend if dosed neat (100%). By preparing a 1% or 10% working dilution, a single drop or 0.05g on your scale delivers a delicate dewy petal or a buttery suede whisper, rather than a choking chemical fire.

The Big Three Perfumery Solvents:
• DPG (Dipropylene Glycol): The universal workhorse solvent. Highly polar, odorless, and water-soluble. Exceptional at dissolving crystalline powders (Vanillin, Coumarin, Evernyl, Ambroxan) and diluting volatile liquids. Note: DPG struggles to dissolve very sticky, non-polar natural tree resins and heavy terpene mixtures.
• TEC (Triethyl Citrate): An ester derived from citric acid. Virtually odorless, biodegradable, and slightly less polar than DPG. The undisputed king for dissolving stubborn, rock-hard natural resins, gums, and absolutes (e.g. Labdanum, Benzoin, Myrrh). Also widely used in clean/eco-certified perfumery.
• IPM (Isopropyl Myristate): An oily, fast-spreading ester with a non-greasy, silky skin-feel. Non-polar, making it ideal for non-alcoholic roll-ons, oil-based perfumes, and reed diffusers. Watch out: IPM has limited solubility in high-water hydroalcoholic blends and can turn cloudy if alcohol proof is too low.

Myths vs. Reality: What Solvents Really Do (and Don't Do):
❌ Myth: "Adding DPG or TEC acts as a magic 'fixative' that makes your perfume last 12+ hours."
✔️ Reality: Solvents are neutral carriers, NOT fixatives! While heavy glycols slightly lower the vapor pressure of volatile top notes through molecular drag, they cannot transform a 30-minute citrus into an 8-hour base note. Real tenacity comes strictly from the chemical weight and evaporation half-life of your aroma materials (e.g. Iso E Super, Ambroxan, Labdanum), never the solvent.
❌ Myth: "Solvents are completely odorless and invisible to the nose."
✔️ Reality: At high concentrations (over 20–30% of the total concentrate), DPG and TEC introduce a slight physical "dampening" or muting effect on top notes, somewhat flattening their sparkling projection, and can impart a faint sweet-chemical or metallic undertone if old or oxidized.

When to Use MORE vs. LESS Solvent:
• Use MORE Solvent (High Dilution, e.g. 1% or 0.1%): For trace powerhouses (Pyrazines, Aldehydes C-10/C-12, Damascenone, Geosmin, IBQ, Birch Tar) where your analytical scale ($0.001\text{g}$) cannot physically measure a small enough neat quantity for a 5g or 10g batch.
• Use LESS Solvent (Neat or High Concentration, 50% to 100%): For structural bulk materials (Hedione, Iso E Super, PEA, Bergamot, Florol). Avoid over-diluting your entire concentrate with DPG/TEC—excess carrier solvent "dilutes" your finished fragrance twice over once final perfumer's alcohol (ethanol) is added, reducing sillage throw and making the fragrance feel muddy.

6. Why is a single raw material described with multiple odor facets?

Raw aroma materials are not single-color paint swatches; they are multi-faceted prisms. Whether a discrete organic molecule or a complex natural botanical extract, almost every material triggers multiple olfactory receptor pathways simultaneously.

Mastering these secondary and tertiary facets is the perfumer's superpower: it allows a single bottle to act as an invisible "chameleon bridge" linking completely different fragrance families.

Classic Multi-Faceted Examples & Their Bridging Powers:
• Coumarin:
— Facets: Toasted almond, sweet tonka marzipan, sun-dried warm hay, and fresh herbal shaving foam.
— Bridging Function: Connects the herbal-citrus top of a Fougère (via its hay/herbal facet) to the rich vanilla-labdanum heart of an Amber (via its sweet toasted almond facet).

• Bergamot FCF:
— Facets: Sparkling zesty lime, bitter-earl-grey tea tannins, spicy floral lavender (due to high linalyl acetate), and a subtle peppery woodiness.
— Bridging Function: Bridges an airy citrus opening into a floral heart (via linalool/linalyl acetate) while simultaneously anchoring into a woody chypre base (via its bitter-tea peel facet).

• Labdanum Resinoid (Rockrose):
— Facets: Sticky golden amber, dark worn saddlery leather, plum wine sweetness, smoky cellar dampness, and warm animalic musk.
— Bridging Function: Provides the leathery dark contrast in a Chypre, the sacred meditative smoke in an Incense Accord, and the rich balsamic glue in Ambre 83.

• Patchouli (or fractionated Clearwood®):
— Facets: Damp cellar earth, dark bitter chocolate / cocoa powder, dry camphoraceous wood, and warm leafy herbal tea.
— Bridging Function: Its earthy facet roots mossy Chypres; its chocolate-cocoa facet bridges gourmand ambers (like Angel); its dry woodiness stabilizes clean modern woody florals.

• Isobutyl Quinoline (IBQ):
— Facets: Green snappy galbanum stem, peaty islay-whisky birch tar, bitter vetiver earth, and raw cured leather jacket.
— Bridging Function: Bridges the razor-sharp green top notes of 1940s vintage chypres (Cellier's Bandit) directly into smoky mossy leather bases.

• Phenylethyl Alcohol (PEA):
— Facets: Sweet honeyed garden rose petal, crisp green stem water, and a warm waxy/yeasty wine cellar undertone.
— Bridging Function: Bridges fresh citrus top notes into heavy balsamic drydowns without ever smelling sharp or synthetic.

Perfumery Rule of Thumb: When an accord feels disconnected or jarring, look for a material that shares a facet with both sides to weld the transition together seamlessly.
